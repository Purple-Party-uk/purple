## The problem

Nobody knows how many people live in the UK without legal status. Many have been here for years, working and raising children.

Most did not arrive in a small boat. They came on a visa and stayed after it ran out.

Finding and removing them one by one would cost more than the state could ever recover. Leaving them where they are keeps them outside the tax system. It also leaves them open to exploitation, because an employer who knows a worker cannot go to the authorities can pay them what they like.

## What we would do

We would run a single, one-off scheme to bring people who have built their lives here into the system. Successful applicants would get legal status, pay tax and move onto the ordinary route to settlement.

The scheme happens once. After it closes, the ordinary rules apply to everyone.

## How it works

### Who qualifies

Anyone living in the UK without legal status who can show they have built, or have started to build, a life here.

There is no cut-off date. A fixed date either includes people who arrived last week or shuts out people who missed it by a few weeks.

Some people are excluded:

- anyone convicted of a serious criminal offence;
- anyone who entered or gained status through fraud;
- people whose short-term visa ran out only recently. Someone who has overstayed a holiday visa by a few months has not built a life here, and can use the ordinary visa system.

### Evidence

A caseworker weighs the evidence as a whole. That can include tenancy agreements, employer records (including informal ones), children's school records, NHS records and ties to the community. Nobody needs the kind of paperwork that only someone with legal status could have.

### The process

A dedicated unit handles applications during a fixed window. The scheme will not be announced in advance with a closing date, to avoid a rush to arrive before it opens. That would not work anyway, because a new arrival cannot show a life already built.

Successful applicants get leave to remain and move onto the five-year route to settlement. The years they have already spent here count towards it. Like work visa holders, they pay a small surcharge on their earnings for five years, which covers everything up to citizenship. Read more on our [work, visas and citizenship]({{ '/policies/immigration/work-visas/' | relative_url }}) page.

Anyone granted status who later commits a serious offence loses it, and is treated like anyone else facing removal.

### The risk

A "building a life" test is fairer than a cut-off date, but it is harder to apply consistently. When thousands of caseworkers make judgements, some decisions will be uneven, and there will be appeals. Those appeals land on tribunals that are already stretched. The scheme will need clear guidance for caseworkers to keep that under control.

## What it costs

We have not put a figure on this policy, because nobody knows how many people would apply.

- **Processing applications** needs a dedicated unit and caseworkers, and some cases will go to appeal.
- **New tax revenue.** People who gain status pay income tax and National Insurance on work that is now off the books, plus the visa surcharge for five years.

We would rather say we do not know than give a number nobody can stand behind.

## Who it affects

**People living here without status** get one chance to come into the system, work legally, pay tax and stop living in fear.

**Their children** can grow up without the threat of a parent being removed.

**Workers and honest employers** gain, because people who can report abuse are much harder to exploit, and employers who rely on them lose their advantage.

**The public finances** gain tax from work that now goes untaxed.
