## The problem

The monarchy is paid for by the public, but much of it runs outside the rules that apply to everyone else.

The Sovereign Grant gives the Royal Household around £86 million of public money a year [CHECK: figure for the current year]. On top of that, the King keeps the profits of the Duchy of Lancaster and the Prince of Wales keeps the profits of the Duchy of Cornwall. Both estates are held because of the Crown. Nobody bought them with their own money. Yet their income is treated as private.

The monarch pays income tax under a voluntary agreement, not because the law requires it. Property passing from one monarch to the next pays no inheritance tax. The Royal Household is outside the Freedom of Information Act, and many other Acts of Parliament do not bind the Crown unless they say so.

Parliament also has to ask the monarch's permission, known as King's Consent, before it can debate a bill that touches the Crown's interests. [The Guardian has reported](https://www.theguardian.com/uk-news/series/queens-consent) that this process was used to seek changes to legislation before the public ever saw it.

Underneath all of this sits a simpler problem. When a monarch dies, the next in line takes over automatically. At no point is anyone asked whether the arrangement should continue.

## What we would do

We would make the monarchy a public office: paid a salary, taxed like everyone else, subject to the law and answerable to Parliament.

- The Duchies of Lancaster and Cornwall, the Royal Collection and the royal palaces move into the Crown Estate, owned by the public and managed by the Treasury.
- The Sovereign Grant ends. The monarch is paid a salary of £750,000 a year and the heir £150,000, both rising with inflation and both taxed.
- The monarch and the Royal Family pay every tax that everyone else pays, with no exemptions.
- The Royal Household becomes a public body, audited and open to Freedom of Information requests, with its staff employed as civil servants.
- Royal Assent, King's Consent and the Privy Council are abolished. Ministers and Parliament take over their functions.
- Letters and meetings between the monarch and ministers about legislation and policy are published within 14 days.
- When a monarch dies, both Houses of Parliament vote on whether the monarchy should continue.

This is not a plan to abolish the monarchy. It is a plan to make it earn its place. If Parliament votes to keep it, the heir becomes monarch on these terms. If Parliament votes to end it, there is an orderly route to whatever comes next.

We know this is a radical policy. We think the case for it is simple: an institution funded by the public should be accountable to the public.

## How it works

**Property.** On the day the Act comes into force, the Duchies, the Royal Collection and Crown property such as Buckingham Palace, Windsor Castle and Holyroodhouse transfer to the Crown Estate. Existing leases and contracts carry on unchanged, so tenants of the Duchies keep their homes, farms and businesses on the same terms. The Crown Estate can keep using the Duchy names commercially. Balmoral and Sandringham, which the Royal Family owns privately, stay in their hands but are taxed like any other private property.

**Housing.** The monarch can live at Buckingham Palace without charge, in the same way the Prime Minister lives at 10 Downing Street. Any other use of public property by the Royal Family for private purposes is charged at the market rate, and the payments are published every year.

**Pay and staff.** The monarch and the heir are the only members of the Royal Family paid from public funds by right. The Royal Household can employ other royals only where it is in the public interest and a non-royal candidate could not do the job as well. Every such appointment, with its salary range and expenses, is published every quarter. Charitable patronages and private engagements are not paid for. Existing Royal Household and Duchy staff transfer with their terms and conditions protected.

**Parliament.** A bill becomes law once both Speakers certify that it has passed Parliament. The Crown Reform Act itself is the last bill to receive Royal Assent. Orders in Council are made by ministers and laid before Parliament. The monarch can respond to public consultations like anyone else, but gets no early sight of draft bills and no private route to change them. The weekly audience with the Prime Minister stays private.

**Succession.** Within 28 days of a monarch's death, each House votes on the motion "That the monarchy should continue." The heir does not take the throne until the votes are counted. If both Houses vote yes, the heir becomes monarch. If either House votes no, the monarchy ends. A joint sitting of both Houses then elects an Interim President within seven days by secret ballot. The government must publish proposals for a permanent settlement within six months, consider whether to hold a referendum, and bring forward legislation within a year.

## What it costs

These figures come from the bill's explanatory notes and are estimates.

| | Per year |
|---|---|
| Sovereign Grant abolished | saves around £86 million |
| Salaries for the monarch and heir | around £900,000 |
| Royal Household budget, voted by Parliament | £30–50 million |
| New tax paid by the Royal Family | £2–5 million |
| **Net saving to the public** | **around £35–55 million** |

Setting up the new Royal Household and completing the property transfers would cost an estimated £5–10 million once. Crown Estate profits already go to the Treasury and would continue to do so.

The Royal Household budget is set by Parliament each year, so the final figure depends on what Parliament decides the monarch's official duties require.
## Who it affects

**The monarch and the heir** receive a fixed, taxed salary in place of the Sovereign Grant and Duchy income, and their dealings with ministers become public.

**Other members of the Royal Family** receive no public money because of who they are. They can still be employed on official duties where that serves the public.

**Royal Household and Duchy staff** move to public employers with their jobs and terms protected.

**Tenants of the Duchies** keep their leases. Their landlord becomes the Crown Estate.

**Taxpayers** pay less and can see exactly what they are paying for.

**Parliament**, and through it the public, decides at each succession whether the monarchy continues.
