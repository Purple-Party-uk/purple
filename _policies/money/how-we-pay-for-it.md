## In short

- The UK government creates the pounds it spends. It cannot run out of them the way a family can.
- The real limit is what the country can make and do. If spending outruns that, prices rise.
- Tax has two jobs: keeping prices steady and deciding who carries the load.
- Britain already borrows £132 billion a year. Our full costings will be published policy by policy.
- We bring spending in step by step, and we spend first on the things that let Britain make more.
- If prices run too hot, a brake written into law kicks in automatically, with an independent referee.
- We cut the interest bill by ending risk-free payouts to banks and keeping more interest in British pension pots.
- Most money is created by private banks when they lend. The Debt Jubilee cancels that debt. It does not put a mountain of new money into people's pockets.

## Britain already spends more than it taxes

In the year to March 2026, the government spent [£132 billion more than it collected](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/governmentpublicsectorandtaxes/publicsectorfinance/bulletins/publicsectorfinances/march2026) in tax and other income. Britain did not go bust. Nobody's pension vanished, and the shops stayed open.

This is normal. The UK has run a deficit in almost every year of the last fifty. Governments of every party have done it, including those that promised to "balance the books".

So the question is not whether a government can spend more than it taxes. It plainly can, and it does. The question is how much, on what, and what keeps prices steady while it does.

## Why a government is not a household

A household earns pounds and spends them. If it spends more than it earns, it has to borrow, and eventually the bank says no.

The UK government is different, because it issues the pound. When it pays a nurse or builds a road, the Bank of England credits the right bank accounts. The money is created at that moment. When you pay tax, money is taken back out of the economy.

Economists call this way of looking at government spending Modern Monetary Theory. You don't need the label. You only need to know that the government cannot run out of pounds, and that this does not mean it can spend without limit.

## Banks create money too

People worry about the government "printing money". Most new money in Britain is not created by the government at all. It is created by private banks.

When a bank gives you a mortgage or a loan, it does not hand over someone else's savings. It types a new number into your account. That money did not exist a moment before. The Bank of England has explained this plainly: [most money in the economy is created by banks when they make loans](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/quarterly-bulletin/2014/q1/money-creation-in-the-modern-economy).

It works in reverse too. When you pay a loan back, that money disappears. A loan creates money, and repaying it destroys money.

So money is created all the time, mostly by banks, mostly as debt that households must pay back with interest. Every mortgage, credit card and car loan added to the money supply when it was taken out. The question that matters is not whether money is created. It is who creates it, and whether it is used to make things or to take from them.

## Why the Debt Jubilee does not flood the economy with money

The Debt Jubilee pays off up to £100,000 of each adult's debt, starting with the home they live in. People hear that and assume the government is pouring hundreds of billions of new pounds into the economy. It isn't, and the reason is the point above.

When the Jubilee pays off your mortgage, the government pays your lender and the loan is cancelled. The money that loan created when it was taken out has already been spent, years ago, on buying the house. The Jubilee does not put new money in your pocket. It replaces a private debt with public money held by your bank. The amount of money people have in their accounts does not jump on the day the debt is cleared.

The real effect comes later. Normally, as people pay their loans back month by month, that money is destroyed bit by bit. After the Jubilee, people keep that money instead. That is where the risk lies, and we are straight about it.

- **Monthly repayments stop.** A family that no longer pays a mortgage has more to spend each month. That extra spending builds up over time. That is why the Jubilee starts in year two, and why the Inflation Brake Rule exists.
- **Money left over goes into a pension pot, not your pocket.** If your debts come to less than £100,000, the rest goes into your Sovereign Wealth Fund pension pot. It stays there until pension age. People over pension age get their debts cleared, but any money left over is not paid out.
- **Some landlords receive cash.** Under rent-to-buy, Jubilee payments that clear what tenants owe go to landlords and their lenders. That money can be spent, and we count it.
- **Banks end up holding more money at the Bank of England.** Banks do not lend more just because they hold more of this money. They lend when people want to borrow and the rules allow it. Under our interest plan, banks will not earn full interest on all of it either.

The Jubilee does not create a mountain of new spending money. It wipes out debt that banks created, and gives people back the income that went on repaying it.

## The real limit: what Britain can make and do

Pounds are not scarce. Nurses, builders, engineers, homes, power stations and factories are.

If the government tries to buy more than the country can produce, it ends up competing with everyone else for the same workers and materials. Prices go up. That is inflation, and it is the real danger of overspending. It hits people on low and fixed incomes hardest.

Inflation was [3.5% in September 2026](https://www.professionalpensions.com/news/4537636/uk-inflation-jumps-forecast-35-dose-reality), pushed up mainly by fuel and transport costs. We take that seriously. A party that says deficits don't matter at all is being as dishonest as one that says every pound must be balanced.

## What tax is for

If tax doesn't pay for spending in the household sense, why tax at all? Tax does two jobs.

**It keeps prices steady.** Tax takes money out of the economy. That leaves room for public spending without pushing prices up. If prices start running too hot, raising tax or slowing spending cools things down.

**It decides who carries the load.** Every tax system chooses who gives up spending power so that the country can afford schools, hospitals and defence. Today that load falls on people who work. We move it onto wealth that is taken out of the economy without making anything: large financial portfolios, expensive property, commercial land, and companies that pay out to shareholders instead of investing. That is why most people earning under £80,000 pay less under our plans, and people living off assets pay more.

## How we keep prices steady

**We spend first on what lets Britain make more.** Research, skills, homes and the things we build ourselves add to what the country can produce. Spending that grows capacity does far less to push up prices than spending that only adds demand.

**We bring changes in step by step.** The Debt Jubilee starts in year two, after a year of setting up. Civil service pay rises over five years. New civilian research funding is phased in over five years. Nothing hits the economy all at once.

**We put the brake in law.** Under our Inflation Brake Rule, the brake is not left to a minister's judgement.

- **The trigger.** Underlying inflation, which leaves out energy and food prices, stays above 3.5% for two quarters in a row.
- **The referee.** An independent Fiscal Council, which replaces the Office for Budget Responsibility, confirms the trigger has been met. It also decides whether the cause is spending at home or a shock from abroad.
- **If the cause is at home,** the government must act within 60 days, using measures agreed in advance and in this order. First, slow down programmes that are still being phased in. Second, raise contributions into everyone's Sovereign Wealth Fund pension pot for a set period. That money is not lost. It comes back to you as savings. Third, add a temporary surcharge on the top income tax bands and on large financial portfolios.
- **If the cause is abroad,** such as a jump in energy prices, we respond with targeted measures such as price caps, windfall taxes and help with essentials. Raising taxes on everyone would only make families poorer without fixing the cause.
- **What is never cut.** The £18,000 income floor and the payment for each child are never used as a brake.
- **It works both ways.** If inflation stays below 1% and unemployment is rising, the brake comes off.

The Bank of England still sets interest rates, but as a backstop. Its main job becomes keeping borrowing costs low and steady. Higher interest rates hit mortgage holders, renters and small businesses first, so we would rather use tax and timing.

**We tackle the costs that come from extraction.** A large share of what people pay goes on rent and interest, not on things being made. Rent-to-buy turns rent into ownership, and the Debt Jubilee clears household debt. These reduce what families pay to people who own things, not to people who make things.

## What will our plans cost?

Britain already borrows [£132 billion a year](https://www.ons.gov.uk/economy/governmentpublicsectorandtaxes/publicsectorfinance/bulletins/publicsectorfinances/march2026). We are finishing our full costings and will publish them policy by policy, including the parts we are less sure about. Until those figures have been checked, we would rather not give you a number we cannot stand behind.

## "Didn't this go wrong in 2022?"

Two things happened in 2022 that people often point to.

The first is inflation, which [peaked at 11.1% in October 2022](https://www.financecalendar.com/uk-cpi-report/). That was driven mainly by energy prices after Russia invaded Ukraine, and by supply problems after the pandemic. Some economists think government support during the pandemic added to it, and that debate is worth having. But a war that doubled gas prices was not caused by a deficit.

The second is the September 2022 mini-budget. Markets reacted badly to large tax cuts for the highest earners, with no costings, no independent check, and no plan to grow what the country could produce, announced while the Bank of England was trying to bring inflation down. We do the opposite. We tax the top more, we will publish our costings, and we phase our spending in.

## What about the interest bill?

Government debt carries interest. Last year the government paid [£97.6 billion](https://scottishfinancialreview.com/2026/04/23/uk-borrows-132bn-in-year-as-debt-tops-2-9-trillion/) in interest on its debt. That is real money, much of it paid to banks and large investors.

We cut that bill in four ways:

- **Stop paying full interest on all the money banks hold at the Bank of England.** Today banks earn the full Bank Rate, risk-free, on hundreds of billions of pounds. Like the central banks of the eurozone and Japan, we would pay the full rate on only part of it.
- **Stop selling the Bank of England's government bonds at a loss.** Taxpayers cover those losses. The bonds will be held until they are repaid.
- **Stop issuing bonds whose interest rises with inflation.** Those bonds caused most of last year's £12 billion jump in interest. We would issue ordinary bonds instead.
- **Keep more of the interest in Britain.** More of the government's borrowing will come from our Sovereign Wealth Fund and from National Savings, so that interest goes into British people's pension pots instead of to overseas funds.

We will keep selling government bonds. Pension funds rely on them, and a sudden change would cause exactly the market panic people worry about.

## What this means for you

When a politician asks "how will you pay for it?", they are asking the wrong question. The right questions are whether Britain has the people, skills and materials to do it, and who should give up spending power so that prices stay steady.

Our plans answer both. We spend on things that make Britain more productive, we bring them in at a pace the country can handle, and we ask those who take from the economy without adding to it to carry more of the load.
