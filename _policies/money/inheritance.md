## The problem

Inheritance Tax is charged on the estate of the person who has died, not on the people who receive it. That makes it easy to plan around. Gifts made more than [seven years before death](https://www.gov.uk/inheritance-tax/gifts) are usually tax-free, so families with money and good advice pass it on early. Families who cannot plan that far ahead, or who lose someone suddenly, pay in full.

The tax also frightens people it should not touch. Families worry that they will have to sell the family home, the farm or the business to pay the bill.

## What we would do

We would replace Inheritance Tax with a lifetime allowance. Each person can receive £400,000 in gifts and inheritances over their whole life without paying tax.

Above that, tax is charged on what you receive.

| Amount received above £400,000 | Rate |
|---|---|
| First £500,000 | 20% |
| Next £500,000 | 30% |
| Next £1 million | 40% |
| Above that | 50% |

Some things are never taxed:

- **Partners.** Gifts and inheritances between husbands, wives and civil partners are tax-free. So are those between couples who have lived together for at least two years.
- **Ordinary gifts.** Birthday and Christmas presents, wedding gifts up to £5,000, and regular gifts out of income that do not leave the giver worse off.

And some things never have to be sold:

- **The family home**, if you inherit it and make it your only home.
- **A working farm or family business**, if you inherit it and keep it running.

You still owe the tax on these. But while you live in the home or keep the farm or business going, you pay a small yearly amount instead of a lump sum. The full bill is due only if you sell.

## How it works

### A running total, not a seven-year rule

Every gift or inheritance above the everyday limits is recorded against the person who receives it, from the day the system starts. Because there is a running total, there is no seven-year rule to game. A home, farm or business handed over during someone's lifetime is taxed in the same way as one left in a will.

### Inheriting your parents' home

The home qualifies if:

- you are the child, stepchild or adopted child of the person who left it, or their grandchild and your parent has died;
- it becomes your only home. If you already own a small home, you have 18 months to sell it.

You do not have to sell to pay the tax. The bill is worked out on the day you inherit. It rises with inflation each year, so it keeps its value. You pay 2% of your income towards it each year.

Here is an example. An heir with their full allowance inherits a £1.5 million house and makes it their only home. The tax on the £1.1 million above the allowance is £290,000. On an income of £40,000, they pay £800 a year. If they sell ten years later, they pay the remaining balance, about £282,000 in today's money.

The remaining balance is due if you sell, move out, buy another home or let the property.

### Inheriting a working farm or family business

The farm or business qualifies if:

- it is a real working farm or trading business, with real work, staff or trade. Holding companies, property portfolios and passive investments do not qualify;
- it passes to a child, stepchild, adopted child or grandchild, or to wider family if there are none;
- you keep it running.

There is no size limit. A large farm qualifies on the same terms as a small one. If you already run a business of your own, you do not have to give it up.

The bill works in the same way as for a home. It is fixed on the day you inherit, rises with inflation, and you pay 2% of your income towards it each year.

- **Retiring does not trigger the bill.** You can step back from the work and keep the farm.
- **Passing it on does not trigger the bill either.** When you hand the farm or business to the next generation, during your life or in your will, the unpaid balance passes with it. Your children carry it on under the same rules.
- **Diversifying is fine.** A farm shop, a campsite or solar panels do not end the arrangement, as long as the business stays in the family. Their profits are taxed like any other business's.
- **Letting some land is fine.** You can let fields, grazing or buildings while the family farms the rest. You can also use a contractor to do the work, as long as the farm business stays yours.
- **Letting the whole farm ends the arrangement.** If the family stops farming and lets all the land to someone else, it has become a landlord, and the remaining balance is due, as it would be for a let house. The one exception is a hard time: if the heir is a child, seriously ill or needs time to take over, the family can let the whole farm for up to five years without losing the arrangement.
- **Selling part of it means paying part of the bill.** If you sell a field, you pay the share of the bill that goes with that field. The rest carries on as before.
- **Selling up means paying the rest.** If the family sells the farm or business, or closes it, the remaining balance is due.

Here is an example. An heir with their full allowance inherits a farm worth £4 million. The tax on the £3.6 million above the allowance is £1.45 million. On a farm income of £30,000, they pay £600 a year. As long as the family keeps farming, that is all they pay. If they sell, the remaining balance is due.

### What this means compared with today

From April 2026, farms and businesses worth more than £2.5 million pay Inheritance Tax on the value above that, at an effective rate of 20%. The bill can be spread over ten years, but it has to be paid whether the family keeps farming or not.

Under our plan, the bill on a large farm is higher on paper, because there is no relief. But a family that keeps farming pays far less in cash, year by year. The bill falls due only if the land is sold.

### A bill that may never be paid off

On a valuable home or a large farm, 2% of income a year will not clear a bill that rises with inflation. In practice, the payment carries on for as long as the family keeps the home or the farm, and passes down with it.

We think that is fair. Nobody has to sell. Nobody pays more than 2% of their income. And if the family does sell, the tax that was put off is paid.

The tax is put off, not cancelled. Any rise in value after you inherit is taxed as income when you sell, like any other gain.

### Hardship

If your income collapses, for example after a failed harvest, you can pause the yearly payments once, for up to 12 months in total, without losing the arrangement.

### Debt Jubilee money

Jubilee money you give to family, or leave when you die, counts towards the £400,000 lifetime allowance. Any tax due is taken from the bonds themselves.

### Hard cases and fraud

Hard cases go to a panel of a tax specialist, a business valuer and an independent chair. Their decisions are published without names, so everyone can see how the rules are applied.

Anyone who hides other property or businesses to claim the arrangement loses all the inherited assets and faces the full tax, penalties and possible prosecution.

## What it costs

We estimate the new system would raise £13–15 billion a year, compared with £7 billion from Inheritance Tax today. Letting families keep homes and farms delays some of this money until the asset is sold, but does not reduce it, because the unpaid bill rises with inflation.

## Who it affects

**Most families** will pay nothing. A typical home left to two children is worth well under £400,000 each.

**Surviving partners** never pay, whether married or not, once they have lived together for two years.

**People who inherit large fortunes** pay more than today, because there are no longer reliefs and trusts to shelter them.

**Families who want to keep a home** can keep it, as long as they live in it.

**Farming families** never have to sell to pay the tax. They pay 2% of their income each year while they farm, and the bill passes to the next generation with the land.

**Families who sell inherited land or property** pay the tax that was put off.
