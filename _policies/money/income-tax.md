## The problem

Today, how much tax you pay depends on how you got your money, not how much you have.

Wages pay income tax and National Insurance. Above £50,270, that is 40% income tax plus 2% National Insurance. Profits from selling shares or a second property are taxed under separate rules, at [18% or 24%](https://www.gov.uk/capital-gains-tax/rates). Dividends have their own rates again. So a person who earns £80,000 by working can pay a higher rate than a person who makes £80,000 from owning things.

The system is also hard to follow. Between £100,000 and £125,140, people lose their tax-free allowance step by step, which creates a [hidden tax rate of 60%](https://www.gov.uk/income-tax-rates/income-over-100000). Few people know it exists until they hit it.

At the very top, some people avoid income tax altogether. They borrow against their shares or property, live on the loan, and let the debt be repaid from their estate when they die. None of it is ever taxed as income.

## What we would do

We would replace four taxes with one. Income tax, employee National Insurance, capital gains tax and dividend tax would become a single income tax.

All income would be taxed the same way: wages, self-employed profits, dividends, gains from selling assets, rent, interest and pensions.

| Income | Rate |
|---|---|
| £0 – £20,000 | 0% |
| £20,001 – £50,000 | 20% |
| £50,001 – £80,000 | 30% |
| £80,001 – £120,000 | 40% |
| £120,001 – £150,000 | 50% |
| Over £150,000 | 60% |

Everyone gets the first £20,000 tax-free. There is no marriage allowance and there are no special exemptions.

## How it works

Each rate applies only to the part of your income inside that band, as it does today. Someone earning £30,000 pays nothing on the first £20,000 and 20% on the last £10,000.

Here is what that means, compared with what someone earning a salary pays today in income tax and National Insurance.

| Income | Tax today | Tax under Purple | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| £25,000 | £3,480 | £1,000 | £2,480 less |
| £30,000 | £4,880 | £2,000 | £2,880 less |
| £40,000 | £7,680 | £4,000 | £3,680 less |
| £50,000 | £10,480 | £6,000 | £4,480 less |
| £80,000 | £23,043 | £15,000 | £8,043 less |
| £120,000 | £43,843 | £31,000 | £12,843 less |
| £150,000 | £58,714 | £46,000 | £12,714 less |
| £250,000 | £105,714 | £106,000 | £286 more |

If you sell shares, a business or a second property for more than you paid, the profit counts as income in the year you sell. It is taxed at the same rates as wages.

A single tax only works if people cannot slip around it. So perks from your own company or trust, money paid out of trusts, and large loans used to live on instead of an income are all taxed as income. [Taxes on wealth and assets]({{ '/policies/money/wealth/' | relative_url }}) explains how.

## What it costs

We estimate the new income tax would raise £527–580 billion a year.

It replaces employee National Insurance (£165 billion), capital gains tax (£15 billion) and dividend tax (£15 billion), as well as today's income tax. Those figures are for the taxes as they stand.

## Who it affects

**People who earn a wage or salary** pay less at every income up to about £250,000. Someone on £30,000 keeps nearly £2,900 more a year.

**People whose income comes from owning things**, such as shares, property or a business they sell, pay the same rates as people who work. For most of them, that means paying more than they do now.

**People who live on borrowed money** against large assets will find that borrowing taxed as income.

Scotland sets its own income tax rates today, so the comparison above is for England, Wales and Northern Ireland.
