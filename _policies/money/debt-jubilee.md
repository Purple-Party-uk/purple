## The problem

Most money in Britain is created by banks when they lend. When a bank gives you a mortgage, it does not hand over someone else's savings. It creates the money in your account, and you spend the next 25 years paying it back with interest.

In early 2026, households owed about £1.75 trillion on mortgages and £253 billion on credit cards, car finance, loans and overdrafts. Every month, a large share of what people earn goes to lenders, not to anything being made. It goes on a debt that banks created at no cost to themselves.

## What we would do

We would pay off up to £100,000 of every adult's household debt, once. Debt on the home you live in is paid first. Whatever is left goes into your pension.

Everyone gets the same £100,000. If you have a mortgage, it pays the mortgage. If you have no debt, it becomes pension savings. Nobody is rewarded for having borrowed, and nobody is left out for having been careful.

The idea comes from the economist Steve Keen's "Modern Debt Jubilee". In his version, every adult gets the same sum, people in debt must use it to pay their debts, and everyone else gets it as savings. We keep that equal treatment, and we turn the savings into pensions.

A jubilee on its own would not last. Debt would build up again within a generation. So it comes with our ban on predatory lending and with [rent to buy]({{ '/policies/home/rent-to-buy/' | relative_url }}), which ends the need to borrow hundreds of thousands of pounds for a home. New mortgages would be paid for by savers, [not by banks creating money]({{ '/policies/home/mortgages/' | relative_url }}).

## How it works

### Who qualifies

Everyone aged 18 or over who lives legally in the UK on the day Purple is elected. That includes British and Irish citizens, people with settled or pre-settled status, people on work and family visas, and refugees. UK residents who happen to be abroad on election day still qualify.

Visitors, people on student visas and people without immigration status do not qualify.

People seeking asylum who are waiting for a decision on election day do qualify, but their money is held until their claim is finally decided. If they are granted protection, it is paid under the normal rules. If the claim fails, the money is cancelled.

### Young people

Children aged 12 to 17 on election day get part of the £100,000, rising with age. It goes into their pension pot, and they can use it towards a rent-to-buy home at any age.

| Age on election day | Amount |
|---|---|
| 12 | £14,300 |
| 13 | £28,600 |
| 14 | £42,900 |
| 15 | £57,100 |
| 16 | £71,400 |
| 17 | £85,700 |

Children under 12 on election day receive nothing. Any cut-off is arbitrary. This one means the biggest gap between children a year apart is about £14,000, not £100,000.

### What gets paid, and in what order

1. **Debt on the home you live in.** Your mortgage, or for rent-to-buy tenants, what you still owe on your home.
2. **Everything else, highest interest first.** Credit cards, car finance, overdrafts, personal loans and buy-now-pay-later.

Arrears count too: council tax, energy and water bills, and rent you owe a landlord. Equity release and lifetime mortgages count up to the amount you originally borrowed, but not the interest that has rolled up on top.

Debts you owe the government, such as benefit overpayments, are written off separately. They do not use up any of your £100,000. Student debt is written off in full, also on top of the £100,000.

The Jubilee never pays buy-to-let mortgages or any other debt on property you let out, business loans, or money you owe friends and family.

### Which balance counts

The Jubilee pays what you owed on election day, or what you owe on the day of payment if that is lower. Borrowing after election day never counts. If you stop repaying after election day, the extra you owe is yours.

Borrowing before the election does count. If you borrow and spend before the election, you have used your Jubilee on that spending. There is no second go.

Lenders cannot cash in either. For any loan taken out after our manifesto is published, the Jubilee repays what was borrowed plus interest at the Bank of England's rate, not the rate in the contract. A bank that pushes expensive loans during the campaign, on the promise that the government will pay them off, gets back what the money cost it and nothing more.

When a debt is paid off, the lender gets what is owed plus interest up to the day of payment. There are no fees, fines or early-repayment charges.

### Couples and shared debts

Your £100,000 is yours. It can pay debts in your own name and debts you share with someone else. It can never pay debts in your partner's name alone.

On a shared debt, such as a joint mortgage, you and your partner decide how much each of your allowances pays. If you cannot agree, each pays half. Each of you makes that choice separately, through your own account. There is no joint form, so nobody can be pressured into signing one.

If your allowance pays more towards a shared home than your partner's does, you own more of the home. This is recorded on the title. If you sell or separate, each of you takes out what your own allowance paid before the rest is divided.

### The money left over

Whatever is left after your debts are cleared goes into your [Sovereign Wealth Fund pension pot]({{ '/policies/money/pensions/' | relative_url }}) as Jubilee Bonds. These are government bonds that cannot be sold. They earn a set return above inflation, so they grow faster than prices.

- **From 60,** you can take out up to one-twentieth of what is left each year. Because it is a share of what is left, it never runs out. It lasts for life, and whatever remains passes to your family.
- **At any age,** you can use it to pay for a rent-to-buy home, including one you move into years after the Jubilee, or towards the deposit on a [mortgage]({{ '/policies/home/mortgages/' | relative_url }}).
- **If you are terminally ill,** you can take it all at once.
- **You can give it to close family** at any time: a spouse or partner, children, stepchildren or grandchildren living legally in the UK. It clears their debts or moves into their pension pot under the same rules. Gifts over £20,000 need an independent witness, to protect older people from being pressured.
- **When you die,** what is left passes to the pension pots of the family members you have named, or to your spouse or partner and then your children if you have named no one. If you have no close family, you can name up to two other people, such as a sibling or a friend.

£50 of every adult's Jubilee becomes their membership share in the new national mutual lender, which every adult in the UK owns. It gives the mutual its starting money, about £2.75 billion in all, and any surplus the mutual makes is paid back into members' pension pots. Read more under [Danish-style mortgages]({{ '/policies/home/mortgages/' | relative_url }}).

Gifts and inherited Jubilee money count towards the receiver's [£400,000 lifetime allowance]({{ '/policies/money/inheritance/' | relative_url }}).

### If your mortgage is cleared

If the Jubilee clears your mortgage or what you owe on your rent-to-buy home, you pay half your old monthly payment into your own pension pot for three years. The money is still yours. It reaches your pocket gradually instead of all at once, which stops a sudden jump in spending pushing up prices.

### Landlords

When a tenant's Jubilee pays for their rent-to-buy home, the landlord's mortgage on that home is paid off in cash. The rest goes into the landlord's pension pot as Jubilee Bonds, under the same rules as everyone else's. Landlords get full value for the share their tenant buys, but as pension savings, not a cash windfall. Company and overseas landlords, who have no pension pot, receive theirs in equal parts over 20 years.

If a tenant's home has not yet joined rent-to-buy when the Jubilee is paid, their share is held for up to two years and paid into the home when it joins.

### When

In year two of a Purple government, after a year of setting up the system that checks who qualifies and what they owe.

## Some examples

| Who | What happens |
|---|---|
| **A couple with a £180,000 joint mortgage.** Alex also has £6,000 on a credit card. | Each allowance pays £90,000 of the mortgage, which is now gone. Alex's card is cleared from Alex's allowance. Alex has £4,000 left and their partner has £10,000, which go into their pension pots. For three years they pay half their old mortgage payment into those pots. |
| **A single renter whose home joins rent-to-buy at £250,000.** They also have £8,000 on cards. | The full £100,000 goes on the home, because the home is paid first. They still owe £150,000, and their monthly payment is about £530 instead of £890. The card debt is not cleared. |
| **A 70-year-old with no debts.** | £100,000 goes into their pension pot. They can take up to £5,000 in the first year, slightly less each year after, for the rest of their life. What is left passes to their family. |
| **A 15-year-old.** | £57,100 goes into their pension pot. They can use it towards a rent-to-buy home when they leave home, or draw on it from 60. |

## What it costs

About £6 trillion, paid once. That is roughly twice the size of the economy. We are not going to pretend otherwise.

| | Estimate |
|---|---|
| £100,000 for about 55 million adults | £5.5 trillion |
| Children aged 12 to 17 | £0.24 trillion |
| Student debt written off | £0.3 trillion |
| **Total** | **about £6 trillion** |

The government creates this money. It does not borrow it from markets. Most of it never reaches anyone's bank account as spending money.

| Where the money ends up | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Mortgages on the homes people live in | £1.0–1.3 trillion |
| Other debts and arrears | about £0.25 trillion |
| Rent-to-buy homes | £0.4–0.8 trillion |
| Jubilee Bonds in people's pension pots | £3.2–4.1 trillion |

Clearing a mortgage does not put new money in anyone's pocket. The money that loan created was spent years ago, buying the house. The bonds in pension pots cannot be sold, so they are not spent all at once either.

The cost comes year by year:

- **The return on Jubilee Bonds.** Each percentage point of return above inflation adds about £36 billion a year to what the state owes savers.
- **What people over 60 take out.** Up to about £70 billion a year at first.
- **Running the scheme.** Checking who qualifies, paying lenders, recording who owns what share of a home, and handling appeals.

### The risk to prices

The real risk is not the £6 trillion. It is what people spend once their repayments stop, and what people over 60 draw from their pots. Together, that could add £130–170 billion a year of spending power. That is 4–6% of the economy each year. If it comes too fast, prices rise.

We control it in three ways. The one-twentieth limit slows what people over 60 can take out. The half-payment rule moves much of the old mortgage money into pension pots for three years. And our [Inflation Brake Rule]({{ '/policies/money/how-we-pay-for-it/' | relative_url }}) is set up with Jubilee spending counted in advance. If prices still run hot, the honest choices are a lower limit, higher taxes at the top, or slowing other Purple programmes. We would rather say that now.

On the standard measure, the national debt roughly triples. We explain why we think that measure misleads in [How we pay for it]({{ '/policies/money/how-we-pay-for-it/' | relative_url }}).

## Who it affects

**Everyone aged 18 or over** gets the same £100,000, as debt cleared or pension savings.

**Renters** start rent-to-buy with a large share of their home already paid for.

**Homeowners with a mortgage** see it cut by up to £100,000 each, or cleared.

**People over 60** get a steady extra income for life.

**Low earners** gain most. A flat £100,000 is worth far more to someone on £20,000 than to someone on £200,000.

**Women** gain on average, because they tend to have smaller pensions. The rules on shared debts mean one partner's allowance can never be used to pay the other's debts.

**Landlords** are paid full value for the share their tenants buy, but as pension savings, not cash. A landlord aged 35 may wait 25 years to draw any of it. Small landlords who lived on their rent keep their tenants' monthly payments, but lose the Jubilee share as income.

**Families with older relatives in the UK** gain more over time, because Jubilee money can be given or left to family. Counting it towards the £400,000 allowance limits how much the wealthiest families gain.

**Children under 12** on election day receive nothing.

**Lenders** are repaid what they are owed, with no fees or penalties. Loans pushed during the campaign are repaid only at the Bank of England's rate.
