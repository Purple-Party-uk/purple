## The problem

Losing your job today means a drop to [Universal Credit](https://www.gov.uk/universal-credit/what-youll-get) or, if you have paid enough National Insurance, [New Style Jobseeker's Allowance](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/new-style-jobseekers-allowance). Both pay the same flat amount whether you earned £20,000 or £60,000. For most people that is a fraction of what they earned, and nowhere near enough to cover the rent or the mortgage.

So people take the first job going, whether or not it fits their skills. Or they pay for private cover, if they can afford it.

## What we would do

Everyone who works would be insured against losing their job, through a fund they pay into and that pays out only to them.

| Months out of work | You get |
|---|---|
| 1 to 6 | 75% of your previous pay |
| 7 to 12 | 50% of your previous pay |

Payments stop after 12 months.

## How it works

**What you pay.** 2.1% of your income above £20,000, collected with income tax and shown as its own line on your payslip. It is one of three [contributions to the Sovereign Wealth Fund]({{ '/policies/money/income-tax/' | relative_url }}). If you earn £20,000 or less, you pay nothing.

**The most you can get.** Your previous pay counts up to 20% above typical full-time pay. At 2025 pay levels, that is £46,850. So the most anyone gets is £35,140 a year for the first six months and £23,425 a year for the next six. The cap is recalculated every year from the latest pay figures.

**The least you can get.** Never less than £18,000 a year, or 90% of your previous pay if that is lower. Nobody gets more out of work than they earned in work. The £18,000 rises with inflation each year.

**Tax.** Payments are taxed as income, like wages.

**Age.** Cover runs until you are 65. At 65 your contribution stops.

**Where the money is kept.** The fund is run by the Sovereign Wealth Fund but kept legally separate from everything else. The law says the money can be spent only on payments and running costs. It can never be handed to the Treasury.

Because people claim most when the economy is doing badly, the fund holds cash and safe, short-term savings, mainly with building societies and mutuals, not shares. Share prices tend to fall at exactly the moment the money is needed.

**Keeping the rate right.** Each year the independent Fiscal Council sets the contribution rate so that the fund holds enough to cover about one bad year, around £10 billion. If the fund has more than it needs, the rate goes down, or the surplus is paid into every contributor's pension pot, in proportion to what they paid in. There are no refunds to people who never claim, because this is insurance. In a deep recession, the fund can borrow from the Treasury at the Bank of England's rate and pay it back from later contributions.

*The full rules on who qualifies are still being finalised.*

## What it costs

We estimate it would cost about £13.7 billion a year at today's level of unemployment, rising to about £23.3 billion in a recession.

It is paid for by the 2.1% contribution, not by taxes. In a normal year, 2.1% covers the cost with a little to spare, and the reserve covers the extra cost of a bad year.

## Who it affects

**Anyone who loses their job** keeps most of their income while they find the next one. Someone earning £30,000 would get £22,500 a year for six months, then £18,000 for six more.

**People who earn £20,000 or less** pay nothing in.

**Higher earners** pay more in than they could ever take out. Above £46,850, extra pay raises the contribution but not the payment.

We are still working out what replaces today's benefits for people this scheme does not cover, including people who have not worked and people out of work for more than a year.
