## The problem

When a British bank gives you a mortgage, it does not lend you someone else's savings. It creates the money by typing a number into your account.

The more banks lend, the more money chases the same homes, and prices rise. Then the bank earns interest for 25 or 30 years on money it created at no cost to itself. Buyers borrow more because prices are higher, and prices are higher because buyers borrow more.

Denmark has done it differently since 1797. Danish homes are paid for by bonds that savers and pension funds buy. The lender arranges the loan and collects the payments, but the money comes from people who already have it.

## What we would do

We would make every new mortgage work the Danish way. When a lender agrees a mortgage, it sells bonds of exactly the same amount, length and interest rate. Your monthly payments pass through the lender to the people who bought the bonds. Lenders would no longer create money for mortgages.

Banks would stop creating money for new mortgages three years after the law is passed. Mortgages you already have are not affected by that date.

We would also set up a national mutual lender, owned by every adult in the UK, so that mortgages are available everywhere.

Our other policies change who needs a mortgage. The [Debt Jubilee]({{ '/policies/money/debt-jubilee/' | relative_url }}) clears most existing mortgages. [Rent to buy]({{ '/policies/home/rent-to-buy/' | relative_url }}) turns renting into owning. Mortgages are still needed to buy a home from someone who lives in it, to build a new home, for rent-to-buy tenants who want to buy the rest of their home in one go, and to improve a home. This policy sets the rules for those mortgages.

## How it works

### Who lends

Any lender licensed under a new Mortgage Credit Act. Mortgage lending has to be done by a separate licensed mortgage lender. A bank can own one, but it is kept apart from the bank's other business, so the bank cannot fund mortgages by creating money. Building societies can apply for a licence like anyone else.

If a borrower cannot pay, the lender covers the loss from its own money. The people who bought the bonds are paid in full. That gives lenders a reason to lend carefully.

### Who buys the bonds

Savers, pension funds, insurers and the [Sovereign Wealth Fund]({{ '/policies/money/pensions/' | relative_url }}). The money people save for their pensions pays for the homes people live in, and the interest goes to savers, not to bank shareholders.

The Sovereign Wealth Fund's share is capped twice:

- it can buy no more than half of any set of bonds, so other buyers, not the state, set the interest rate;
- no more than a quarter of the Fund can be in housing, counting mortgage bonds and the rent-to-buy shares it owns. If it goes over, the Fund reports to Parliament and buys fewer mortgage bonds until it falls back. Its main job is investing in British businesses, and the cap protects that.

### The national mutual lender

Every adult living legally in the UK is a member. Membership cannot be sold or passed on.

- **It plays by the same rules.** It sells bonds to fund its mortgages and cannot create money. It gets no special terms, so nobody can say the state is undercutting private lenders.
- **It is there when others are not.** It must offer mortgages in every part of the UK, and self-build, DIY-build and home improvement loans everywhere. It turns down anyone who fails the standard affordability check. Anyone it turns down for a home purchase can use rent to buy instead.
- **Its starting money comes from the Jubilee.** £50 of each adult's Debt Jubilee becomes their membership share. That raises about £2.75 billion. If more is needed, the government adds a one-off grant, with no votes and no share of the profits.
- **Any surplus comes back to members.** First it builds up the reserves the regulator requires. Then it lowers what it charges borrowers. Anything left is paid into members' pension pots, not as cash.
- **Members run it.** Members elect some of the board directly. A members' council of about 100 people, chosen at random like a jury, appoints the rest and holds the board to account. Ministers have no say in who runs it.
- **It can never be sold.** In the 1990s, building societies such as the Halifax and Northern Rock became banks owned by shareholders. Members took one-off windfalls, and much of the sector was lost. The law would stop that happening to the mutual, whatever members vote.

### The rules for borrowers

- You can borrow up to 90% of the home's value. You can use your Jubilee money towards the 10% deposit.
- You can borrow for up to 30 years.
- Every payment repays some of the loan. Interest-only mortgages are not allowed, except for up to two years while you build your own home.
- Fixed and variable rates are both allowed. Loans that have to be refinanced every year are not.
- If you hit hard times, you can pause or reduce your payments for up to 12 months in total over the life of the loan.
- These mortgages are for homes you live in. Lending to buy homes to let ends.

### The same price for everyone

Every borrower pays the bond rate plus the lender's margin, which covers its costs and the risk of the loan not being repaid. Each year the regulator sets a maximum margin for each band. The bands depend only on the size of the loan compared with the home's value, never on who you are.

| How much you borrow | Highest margin (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Up to 60% of the home's value | 0.75% |
| 60% to 80% | 1% |
| 80% to 90% | 1.5% |
| Self-build, while the home is being built | 2% |

Within a band, everyone pays the same. A lender can turn a loan down, but it cannot charge one borrower more than another for the same loan.

### Paying off early costs nothing extra

You can repay at any time, with no exit fee. You can pay off what you borrowed, or buy back the bonds that fund your mortgage at their market price and hand them to the lender.

The second option protects you when interest rates rise. Bond prices fall when rates rise, so you can clear the loan for less than you borrowed. A family that has to sell in a falling market is less likely to be stuck owing more than the home is worth.

A buyer can also take over the seller's mortgage on the same terms, if the lender accepts them.

### Building and improving homes

A bond needs a finished home behind it. While a home is being built, there is only land and part-finished work, so building is paid for differently.

- **Developers** borrow from banks to build. That is business lending, so banks can still create money for it. We want bank lending going into building things. When the homes are sold, the buyers' mortgages follow the new rules.
- **Self-builders and DIY builders** borrow from licensed lenders, including the mutual. The money is paid out in stages as the home goes up.
- **Home improvements up to £25,000** need no surveyor. Money is paid against receipts for materials and invoices from tradespeople.
- **Home improvements over £25,000** are costed by a surveyor, and the money is paid in stages after each stage is inspected. The lender pays for the inspections, not you.

Your own labour is not lent against. The total loan stays within 90% of what the home is worth once the work is done.

Every bank would have a target for lending to self-builders, set at 5% of its building and mortgage lending. A bank that falls short pays the difference to the mutual, which lends it to self-builders instead. Banks that are good at self-build lend. Banks that are not pay for a lender that is.

### If you cannot get a mortgage, or do not want one

You can buy through [rent to buy]({{ '/policies/home/rent-to-buy/' | relative_url }}). When an owner-occupied home is sold, the buyer puts in whatever they have, such as savings, Jubilee money or what they made from selling their last home, and buys the rest through rent to buy. The seller can be paid at once, with the Sovereign Wealth Fund buying their share, or bought out gradually.

Homes that come in this way pay a fair interest rate, not the 0.5% that applies to homes from the rented stock. Each year the Fiscal Council publishes the average rate on new mortgages, minus expected inflation. That becomes the rate for buyers who join that year, fixed for the whole term. If rent to buy were cheaper than a mortgage, nobody would take a mortgage, and the Sovereign Wealth Fund would end up paying for most home purchases at a loss to its members. With the same cost either way, people can choose what suits them.

### Mortgages you already have

Most are cleared by the Debt Jubilee. Anything above your £100,000 stays with your current lender on your current terms. You can move it into the new system at any time with no early-repayment charge.

Home purchase plans that follow Islamic finance rules can be funded by bonds on the same terms as other mortgages.

## What it costs

This policy costs the public little directly, because mortgages are paid for by the people who buy the bonds, not by the state.

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Starting money for the mutual | About £2.75 billion, taken from the Debt Jubilee. No new public cost. |
| A top-up grant to the mutual, if needed | One-off. Not yet costed. |
| A regulator and licensing | Not yet costed. The UK already regulates similar bonds, which gives a starting point. |
| Bank of England support in a crisis | Nothing unless it is used. The Bank would lend to licensed lenders against their bonds, as it does for banks now. |
| Returns to the Sovereign Wealth Fund | Mortgage bonds earn less than shares but are much safer. This is a cost to pension pots, not the public finances, and the quarter cap limits it. Not yet modelled. |

What comes back: house prices that grow more slowly, interest paid to savers instead of bank shareholders, the mutual's surplus returned to members, and a lower risk of a housing crash that the public has to pay to clean up.

### The honest difficulties

**Ninety per cent loans carry more risk.** Denmark stops at 80% for this kind of bond. Lenders and the mutual carry the extra risk through a higher margin and their own reserves. If more borrowers cannot pay, the mutual's members feel it.

**The mutual has three years to be ready.** It needs a licence, money, staff, systems and a board before banks stop. If it is not ready, the date has to move, or home sales stall. Work on it starts on day one.

**Denmark's system is not flawless.** Before 2008, Danish borrowers moved heavily into interest-only and variable-rate loans, and house prices fell sharply. That is why we would ban interest-only loans and loans that have to be refinanced every year.

**Banks will call the self-build target a tax.** In effect it is one, but only for banks that do not lend to self-builders.

**It is a large change to banking law.** It needs a new Act, a regulator, a new mutual and changes to how the Bank of England supports lenders. Denmark has run this system for over two centuries, so there is plenty to learn from.

## Who it affects

**First-time buyers** gain most. House prices grow more slowly, the deposit is 10%, and Jubilee money can pay it. Anyone who cannot borrow can buy through rent to buy.

**People who already own their home** see slower price rises. If they have a mortgage above their Jubilee, they can keep it or move it into the new system without a charge.

**Every adult** owns part of the national mutual and shares in its surplus through their pension pot.

**Savers and pension funds** get a safe investment, and the interest that banks used to keep.

**Banks** lose the power to create money for mortgages. Their lending moves towards businesses and building, which is where we want it.

**Building societies** can carry on lending under the new licence. The mutual is meant to fill gaps, not to undercut them.

**Women, disabled people and anyone with a lower income** cannot be charged more than anyone else for the same loan.

**Landlords** can no longer take out new mortgages to buy homes to let.
