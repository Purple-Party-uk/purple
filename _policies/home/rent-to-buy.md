## The problem

A tenant can pay rent for thirty years and own nothing at the end of it. Every payment buys the landlord a larger share of a house. None of it buys the tenant anything.

The landlord's return does not come from building or making anything. It comes from owning the home and charging someone else to live in it. When rents rise faster than pay, tenants fall further behind every year, and saving for a deposit gets harder, not easier.

We think a home is somewhere to live. It should not be an investment product paid for by the people living in it.

## What we would do

We would turn almost every rented home into a rent-to-buy home. Each payment would buy the tenant a share of the home they live in. Within 25 years at most, the home would be theirs outright.

Landlords would not be forced to sell. They would keep a share of the home, and that share would shrink as the tenant pays, until it reaches nothing. While it shrinks, it keeps its value.

Every landlord would have to bring their homes into the scheme within 18 months of it opening. There would be no opt-outs.

## How it works

### The price

The price is fixed when the home enters the scheme. It is set at the home's market value or the mortgage still owed on it, whichever is higher. After that, the price no longer follows the housing market.

Each year, what is still owed rises by inflation or by average pay growth, whichever is lower. On top of that, the tenant pays 0.5% interest. That is the only cost of buying over time.

This means **your payments never rise faster than pay does across the country.** It also means the money you pay keeps its value for the person you are buying from.

### The Debt Jubilee gives most renters a large share on day one

Under our [Debt Jubilee]({{ '/policies/money/debt-jubilee/' | relative_url }}), every adult living legally in the UK on election day receives up to £100,000 of debt relief, and debt on the home you live in is paid first. For a tenant in the scheme, that means what they still owe on their home. A single renter starts with up to £100,000 paid off. A couple starts with up to £200,000 paid off.

| A home that enters the scheme at £250,000 | Still owed | Monthly payment |
|---|---|---|
| Without the Jubilee | £250,000 | about £890 |
| A single renter | £150,000 | about £530 |
| A working couple | £50,000 | about £180 |

These payments are in today's money, over 25 years at 0.5% interest. Each year they rise by inflation or pay growth, whichever is lower. A maintenance payment is added on top (see below).

Older renters get the same help. Whatever is left after their debts are cleared goes into their pension pot, like everyone else's.

- If your home has not yet joined rent-to-buy when the Jubilee is paid, your share is held for up to two years and paid into your home when it joins.
- You can use the Jubilee money in your pension pot to pay for your rent-to-buy home at any time, including if you start renting after the Jubilee.
- If the Jubilee clears what you owe on your home, you pay half your old monthly payment into your pension pot for three years.

### No deposits, and your share moves with you

There are no deposits. If a tenant damages the home, the cost comes out of their share of it.

When you move, your share comes with you. You can be bought out straight away, or you can stay in the chain and receive an income as the next tenant gradually buys you out. The next tenant can buy the rest of the home outright or carry on paying monthly. Over time, each home ends up owned by one long-term resident, or shared between the people who have lived there.

Shared houses become co-operatives owned by the people who live in them.

### Buying a home that comes up for sale

Rent to buy is also the way in for anyone who cannot get a [mortgage]({{ '/policies/home/mortgages/' | relative_url }}), or does not want one. When someone sells a home they live in, the buyer can put in whatever they have, such as savings, Jubilee money or what they made from selling their last home, and buy the rest through rent to buy.

The seller can be paid at once, with the Sovereign Wealth Fund buying their share at market value. Or they can keep their share and be bought out gradually, as tenants who move on can now.

These homes pay a fair interest rate instead of 0.5%. Each year the Fiscal Council publishes the average rate on new mortgages, minus expected inflation. That becomes the rate for buyers who join that year, fixed for their whole term. The price still rises only with inflation or pay, whichever is lower.

Homes that come from the rented stock keep the 0.5% rate, whoever the tenant is.

There are two rates for a reason. The 0.5% rate is for people who have rented, often for years, with nothing to show for it. If buying through rent to buy were cheaper than a mortgage, nobody would take a mortgage. The Sovereign Wealth Fund would end up paying for most home sales at a return below what it owes its members, and cheap money would push up the prices homes come in at. With the same cost either way, people can choose what suits them. Owners also have no reason to let a home out just to get it the lower rate, because it is the landlord who loses from that rate.

### Students and emergency housing

Emergency and temporary accommodation and student housing are not rent-to-buy homes, because people do not stay long enough to buy them. Tenants there still build a share while they live there. When they leave, the Sovereign Wealth Fund pays them their share straight away. The Fund is then repaid from future payments on the same home.

Refuges and supported housing, tied homes, social care housing and military housing can also have set end dates. Tenants in all of them build a share.

### If your income falls

The scheme bends rather than breaks. The [£18,000 income floor]({{ '/policies/money/nit/' | relative_url }}) covers most drops in income. If that is not enough, you can apply to a local hardship panel for a lower payment, and the 25 years are extended. Nobody loses their share because their income fell, and nobody is made homeless.

The same national rules apply in every panel:

- the lower payment must at least cover the 0.5% interest, so the amount owed never grows;
- it is tested against your income after the income floor;
- no household pays more than a set share of its income on its home;
- payments return to normal when income recovers.

Lower payments can last up to five years in any ten. After that, a tenant who still cannot afford the home is helped to move to a cheaper one. Their share is cashed out and goes with them, and the council helps with moving costs. Nobody has to move until a suitable, affordable home has been offered.

The five-year limit does not apply to people who cannot raise their income: people over state pension age, disabled people unable to work, full-time carers, people who are terminally ill, and people living in homes adapted for a disability. They can keep paying the lower amount for as long as they need to. If a landlord does not want to wait, the Sovereign Wealth Fund buys their share.

### Maintenance

Every home has a maintenance reserve: money held in a protected account that can only be spent on that home. It pays for buildings insurance and repairs, including the big items that fail without warning, such as a boiler or a roof.

Everyone who owns part of the home pays into the reserve according to their share. A tenant who owns 30% pays 30% of the monthly reserve payment. The landlord, or whoever owns the rest, pays the rest.

A new National Housing Maintenance Agency inspects every home when it enters the scheme and sets the reserve at the right level for it. Older homes, or homes with known problems, need a larger reserve. Spending needs the agreement of everyone who owns part of the home, except urgent safety work, which goes ahead at once. The Agency settles disagreements.

The tenant can arrange repairs or ask the Agency to do it. Work is charged at cost. Where the Agency does the work, it adds no more than 2% for administration, so maintenance cannot become a new way to make a profit. The Agency can step in straight away wherever a home is unsafe.

The reserve belongs to the home, not to the tenant. When someone owns the home outright, it is released to them.

### For landlords

A landlord keeps their share and is repaid as the tenant pays. The share rises with inflation or pay, whichever is lower, and earns the 0.5% interest. Where there is a mortgage, the lender is repaid alongside the landlord.

Much of the money comes back early. When a tenant's Jubilee clears part of what they owe, the landlord's mortgage on the home is paid off straight away. The rest goes into the landlord's pension pot as Jubilee Bonds, not as cash. Company and overseas landlords receive theirs in equal parts over 20 years. On a £250,000 home let to a working couple, up to £200,000 is settled at once.

A landlord who wants out sooner can:

- move their share into their pension pot in the Sovereign Wealth Fund, where it earns exactly what the tenants pay and is locked until pension age; or
- sell it for cash to the Fund, a council, a co-operative or a community trust, at its current value;
- or do a mix of the two.

New buy-to-let lending, where a loan exists only to collect rent, would stop.

### Landlords who hide tenancies

Local enforcement teams would check bank, utility and letting-site records for tenancies that have not been registered. Breaking the rules is not a crime. It is dealt with by a housing tribunal, with a right of appeal, and penalties rise with how serious the breach is: a fine for late registration, and loss of part or all of the landlord's share for deliberate or repeated concealment.

The tenant carries on as normal, and rent paid while the tenancy was hidden counts towards their share. Lenders are not penalised.

## What it costs

We have not yet put figures on this policy. These are the costs it carries.

- **No yearly subsidy.** Because what is owed rises with inflation or pay, repayments keep their value. Shares in the Sovereign Wealth Fund earn exactly what tenants pay. The one gap is in years when pay grows more slowly than inflation. Then landlords and the Fund lose a little. That is a deliberate choice to put tenants first.
- **Money tied up in the Fund.** Paying out students, buying shares from landlords who want cash, and covering tenants on long-term lower payments all tie up the Fund's money. It is repaid, but it earns less than the Fund could make elsewhere. Buying out sellers of owner-occupied homes also ties up the Fund's money, but those homes pay the fair rate, so there is no subsidy.
- **Maintenance.** Where the Fund owns part of a home, it pays its share of that home's maintenance reserve. The cost shrinks as tenants buy the Fund out.
- **Running the scheme.** The registration portal, local enforcement teams, hardship panels, the housing tribunal and the Maintenance Agency. Shares lost through penalties pay for part of the enforcement.

The Debt Jubilee is costed in its own policy. We will publish figures for each of these costs once they have been modelled.

## Who it affects

**Renters** stop paying for someone else's home and start buying their own. Most start with a large share on day one. Nobody needs a deposit, and nobody loses what they have built up by moving for work.

**Landlords** are not forced to sell. They are repaid in full, what they are owed rises with inflation or pay, and much of it comes back early. What they lose is the chance to collect rent forever and to profit when house prices rise.

**Younger people** gain most, because most renters are younger than most landlords.

**Small landlords whose rental home is their pension** keep its value, and can move it into a pension pot that earns what their tenants pay.

**Lenders** are repaid as before. They stop making new loans whose only purpose is to collect rent.

**People who already own their home** can sell to a rent-to-buy buyer and be paid at once by the Sovereign Wealth Fund, or bought out gradually.

**Buyers who cannot get a mortgage** can buy a home that comes up for sale through rent to buy, at a fair interest rate.
